What would “no association” look like?

If role made no difference (H0), Traitors and Faithfuls would reach the final at the same rate as everyone. Overall, 25 of 100 contestants reached the final. Work out each group’s fair share: the expected counts.

Why are 25% expected to reach the final?

It comes from our own data: 25 of the 100 contestants reached the final, so the overall rate is 25%. If role made no difference, knowing whether someone was a Traitor or a Faithful would tell you nothing about their chances. Every contestant would have the same chance as everyone else: the overall 25%.

So under H0, 25% of the Traitors and 25% of the Faithfuls should reach the final. The totals stay the same; only how the finalists are shared between the groups changes.

Observed finalistsExpected finalists if H0 were true

χ² adds up how far each cell is from its fair share. For each cell: (observed − expected)² ÷ expected. The biggest gap is the Traitor finalists: (9 − 5)² ÷ 5 = 3.20. All four cells together give χ² = 5.33.

You will never calculate this by hand. You need the idea for two reasons: SPSS warns you when expected counts are too small (step 6), and it tells you what a big χ² means: our table is far from the “no association” table.

5

Testing the null: build the “just luck” world

A sceptic says: “Role makes no difference. The Traitors just got lucky.” That is H0. To test it, build a world where the sceptic is right: keep the 25 finalists, but hand out the 20 Traitor envelopes at random so role can’t matter. Then see how often luck alone gives a result as extreme as ours (9 Traitor finalists).

Reading it. Each shuffle is one “just luck” version of the game. Luck usually gives about 5 Traitor finalists. Our 9 is 4 above that; 1 would be just as far below, so both count as “at least as extreme”. The share of dark bars is the p-value.

After 1,000 shuffles you should land near 4%. Shuffling like this is exactly what Fisher’s Exact Test does (p = .040). SPSS’s chi-square shortcut gives p = .021. Both are below .05, but they differ because the smallest expected count is only 5, where the shortcut gets rough. That is why the expected-count rule exists (step 6).
